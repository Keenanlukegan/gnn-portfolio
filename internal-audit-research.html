THE SHORT VERSION

An agent's value in internal audit sits almost entirely in the coordination layer — chasing evidence, reconciling what arrived against what was asked for, tracking open findings to closure — and almost not at all in the judgment layer. That is fortunate, because the coordination layer is also where the hours burn. The binding constraint is that an agent may prepare, gather and draft, but it can never be the control: a named human signs, and the standards say so in writing. Everything defensible is built on one thing — a per-action record of what the agent did, on what input, producing what output, reviewed by whom. Build that first and the rest can follow. Build it later and nothing that came before can be relied on.

Six sections, plus a teardown of how the existing internal-audit products actually work and what is worth copying from them. Every regulatory statement was checked against its primary source on 23 August 2026; where a fact could not be verified it is marked as unverified rather than asserted.

02 · WHERE THE HOURS GO

Where the manual hours actually sit.

Ranked by hours burned, not by how impressive the automation sounds.

Read this ranking honestly judgment, not finding

I looked for a published survey that breaks internal-audit hours down by lifecycle stage and did not find one — not for banks, not for Singapore, not at any useful granularity. The ordering below is a professional-judgment ranking built from the shape of the process and from practitioner accounts of where audits stall. Treat it as a hypothesis to test. The first question to ask the audit function is whether their own timesheet data agrees — and that question is itself a good reason to be in the room.

One quantified figure did turn up, worth having in your pocket with its caveats attached. Suralink — a product that does nothing but manage evidence request lists — states that "the average firm spends 20 hours per engagement managing document requests" and sells against exactly that number. Three caveats, all material: it is a vendor's own marketing figure, it describes external audit firms rather than a bank's internal audit function, and it is the number they are selling against. Use it as an order of magnitude and as a question — "what does this cost you per engagement?" — never as a fact.

That is the whole opportunity, and the whole discipline: take the top of the list, leave the bottom alone. A demo that automates risk scoring is impressive and unsellable. A demo that closes twenty overdue findings is unimpressive and buys the room.

04 · THE CONSTRAINT

An agent may prepare, gather and draft. It cannot be the control.

Human sign-off is not a design preference to be optimised away later. It is written into the standard the audit function is measured against, and it is named in every supervisory statement on AI in finance. This is the most valuable page in the document, because it is what tells you what not to promise in a room full of auditors.

Four independent sources, the same answer

  • Evidence must be reperformable, and that is the definition of sufficient. IIA 14.1 defines information as sufficient only when it "can enable a prudent, informed, and competent person to repeat the engagement work program and reach the same conclusions." Confidence in an output is not sufficiency. Reperformability is.
  • Named humans must review and approve. 14.6: internal auditors and the engagement supervisor must review the documentation for accuracy, relevance and completeness, and "the chief audit executive must review and approve" it. Three roles, all human, all named in the workpaper.
  • Reliability comes from independence. 14.1: reliability is strengthened when information is obtained directly by an internal auditor or from an independent source, and when corroborated. An agent's output is not independent of the agent — so an agent can fetch evidence but cannot be it.
  • Oversight has to be real. FSB Sound Practice 10 requires oversight that is "meaningful, i.e. it involves humans having sufficient ability, authority, and incentive to intervene, as opposed to oversight that is nominal or driven by 'tick-the-box' compliance" — and warns specifically about automation bias, the overreliance that sets in when the output is usually right.

What not to promise — and what to say instead

Each pair says the same technical thing. The left version loses the room; the right version is defensible and more impressive to someone who audits for a living, because it shows you know where the line is.

  • ✗ "The agent tests the control."
    ✓ "The agent assembles the population and the evidence. The auditor performs the test and signs it."
  • ✗ "The agent picks the sample."
    ✓ "The agent extracts the complete population, so the auditor can test all of it or select from it."
  • ✗ "The agent closes the issue."
    ✓ "The agent chases, collects and packages the closure evidence. The auditor confirms closure."
  • ✗ "The agent writes the workpaper."
    ✓ "The agent drafts from the test results with every assertion pointing at its source. The auditor reviews, corrects and signs."
  • ✗ "It's fully automated end to end."
    ✓ "No step completes without a named human. What changes is how much is already done when they arrive."

The recursion nobody mentions until the second meeting

Internal audit is the third line of defence — the function that audits everyone else's controls, including the bank's use of AI. The moment IA runs its own agent, that agent lands in the bank's AI inventory and gets a risk-materiality rating like any other, and IA is assuring a tool it depends on. Not a reason to avoid the build. A reason the traceability layer in §06 has to be there from day one: internal audit is the one function in the bank that cannot afford a tool it cannot audit. Expect the question, and answer it before it is asked.

TIER 1 · LEAD WITH THIS

Issue tracking and remediation follow-up

The strongest candidate in the entire lifecycle, and the research moved it above evidence chase. The reason is unusually concrete: Standard 15.2 is the only place in the Standards where the automation is named as good practice. Its Considerations for Implementation say internal auditors "may use a software program, spreadsheet, or system to track whether management's action plans are implemented according to the established timelines", that "a program or system may automate the workflow from risk assessment to action plan completion", and that such a workflow "may include automated emails that notify the appropriate parties regarding action plans that are nearing their target completion dates." You are not asking an audit committee to accept a novel idea. You are offering to do, well, a thing their own standard already tells them to do.

  • What the agent does: holds every open finding with owner, agreed action, target date and risk rating; chases owners on a schedule; escalates on aging thresholds; collects closure evidence and packages it for review; maintains the overdue view and the committee-ready status report.
  • Where the human stays: whether the closure evidence is adequate, and — explicitly reserved to the CAE by 15.2 and 11.5 — whether delay or inaction has accepted a risk beyond tolerance. The agent never closes an issue. It makes closure a one-click decision instead of a three-week retrieval.
  • Why it demos: visible state change in a room. Twenty open findings, six overdue, three chased live, one packaged for closure. It runs between audits, so it produces value on day one rather than at the next audit cycle.
TIER 1 · SECOND

Evidence request and chase

The largest single pool of hours, and almost none of it judgment. Issue the request list, track what was asked versus what arrived, detect wrong-period or wrong-version submissions, chase the owners who have not responded, and hand the auditor a complete, indexed package instead of an inbox.

  • Where the human stays: what to request (that is scoping) and whether what arrived is relevant, reliable and sufficient under 14.1 — a judgment the standard assigns to the auditor in terms. The agent's job ends at "here is everything, indexed, with gaps flagged."
  • The design detail that makes it defensible: the agent must never fill a gap by inference. An unanswered request stays visibly unanswered — 14.1 requires the auditor to decide whether missing evidence is itself a finding, and an agent that silently smooths over a gap destroys that decision.
TIER 2 · SHOW, GATED

Deterministic control testing, and workpaper drafting

The research changed the framing here. The assumption going in was that workpaper drafting simply "needs a review gate". That is not enough, and the reason is 14.6: the test is not whether a reviewer read it, but whether "an informed, prudent internal auditor, or similarly informed and competent person, could repeat the work and derive the same engagement results." A fluent draft that does not trace back to its sources is worse than no draft, because it consumes review attention while producing nothing reperformable — precisely the automation bias the FSB warns about.

  • So the build order inverts. Draft-then-review is the wrong sequence. Trace-then-draft is the right one: every drafted assertion carries a pointer to the artifact, the date and the procedure it came from, and the reviewer can follow any sentence back to its source in one step. Which means workpaper drafting depends on §06 existing first — not a parallel track, the second thing built on the same spine.
  • Deterministic testing — tie-outs, completeness and existence checks, exception listing against a rule the auditor wrote — is safe on one condition: the auditor owns the rule and the conclusion, and the rule is recorded verbatim in the workpaper. The agent executes; it does not decide what counts as an exception. Judgmental testing is not in this tier.
TIER 3 · NEVER LEAD

Anything that forms a judgment

Risk scoring, sample selection, finding severity ratings, and the engagement conclusion. Confirmed by the research and worth stating more sharply than intuition does: these are not merely "riskier" — they are the specific outputs the Standards assign to a named human, and the ones that would attract the highest risk-materiality rating under MAS's draft AI guidelines, with proportionately heavier controls attached.

  • Sample selection determines what does and does not get looked at. 14.1 makes representativeness an auditor judgment. Automating it means an agent decided what the audit never saw — the hardest thing to defend after the fact.
  • Risk scoring scopes the entire annual plan. One weighting error propagates to everything.
  • Severity and conclusions are 14.3 and 14.5 — the auditor's opinion, the exact thing MAS TRM 15.1.1 requires to be independent and objective.
  • The counterintuitive part: these are the most demoable items on the list. Risk scoring looks spectacular in five minutes. That is exactly the trap — it invites the one objection you cannot answer, in front of the people whose professional judgment you just appeared to automate. There is a safe version worth building later: the agent assembles the inputs to a risk assessment (loss history, incident data, prior findings, change volumes) and the auditor scores. Assembly, not scoring.

06 · THE SPINE

Evidence and traceability.

There is exactly one question a bank audit committee will actually ask about an agent, and it is not "how accurate is it." It is: "how do you know what it did?" Every agent action needs an immutable record — what it did, on what input, producing what output, when, under whose authority, and reviewed by which human. Not a log for debugging. An evidence chain, built to the same reperformability standard as the workpaper it supports.

The minimum record, per agent action

  • Action ID, and the engagement and workpaper reference it belongs to — indexed the way the audit file is indexed, not the way a system log is.
  • Timestamp, and the source of the clock.
  • Agent identity and version, plus the version of the prompt, rule or configuration in force at that moment. An agent that changed last Tuesday did different work last Monday.
  • Input artifacts — as immutable pointers or hashes, never copies that can drift out of step with the source.
  • Output produced, verbatim, before any human edit.
  • The human who requested it and the human who reviewed it, with review timestamp and disposition: accepted, corrected, or rejected.
  • Every override — what the human changed and why. The FSB asks for exactly this: tracking when humans override AI recommendations, analysing the patterns, and feeding it back into both the system and the oversight process.
  • Retention aligned to the audit function's own retention policy — 14.6 requires engagement documentation to be retained per law, regulation and the function's policy, typically measured in years.

Why this is the flag worth planting

  • It is what makes everything else admissible. 14.6 requires that a competent person could repeat the work and derive the same result. Where an agent did part of the work, that is only true if the agent's steps are themselves reperformable. Without the spine, every Tier 1 and Tier 2 output is an assertion; with it, each one is evidence.
  • Supervisors are converging on it. The FSB records that banks running agentic systems have added agentic-specific attributes to their AI inventories — "the tools the agent can access, its components, and guardrails to manage risk" — and that more mature organisations do agent 'certification', where an agent is reviewed and approved for use within defined boundaries, with its certified status and permitted scope recorded.
  • MAS's draft expectations point the same way: inventory every AI use case, rate its risk materiality, and document development "to support reproducibility and auditability."
  • It is the honest answer to the recursion in §04. An audit function can only adopt a tool it can audit. The spine is that auditability.

How this sits on the Microsoft platform

The platform gives a real head start, and it is worth saying so plainly in the room. Copilot Studio establishes native agent identities in Dataverse that distinguish agent actions from user actions; agent activity propagates to Purview and Dataverse for audit and admin review; human-in-the-loop approval gates can be required before write operations, with proposed-action previews and dry runs; and Power Platform governance brings DLP policies and environment isolation. Purview retains audit data for up to 180 days, or up to 365 with an E5 licence. Much of the plumbing exists.

What still has to be built is a different artifact, not a better version of the same one. Platform logs answer administrative and interaction questions — who deployed this agent, who changed its settings, who talked to it. An audit engagement needs the evidence chain of one engagement, indexed to a workpaper reference, retained for the audit function's retention period rather than the tenant's log window, and exportable as a self-contained package an external auditor or supervisor can read without being given access to the tenant. That last property matters most and nothing produces it for free — a regulator does not get a Purview seat. So: an engagement-level evidence ledger sitting above the platform's logs and citing them. Complementary, and a clean, well-bounded slice of work.

Where the recommendations in §06 come from

These are not drawn from the audit literature. They come from running automated systems in production and getting caught. In one operation — a tutoring practice in Singapore — a delivery system logged 40 messages as sent; an independent verification process, running on its own schedule and reading the destination rather than the sender's own log, established that 23 of them had never arrived. Every recommendation above follows from that: an agent's own report of its work is not evidence of its work, the record must be written at the moment of action rather than reconstructed afterwards, and something other than the agent has to check. That is the same principle an auditor calls independence, arrived at the expensive way — and the reason this is the section I would build first.

B · Two state machines to copy verbatim

Evidence requests — Suralink

outstanding → fulfilled → accepted | returned

Four states, two actors, one loop: returned sends it back to outstanding. That is the entire mechanism, and it is enough.

  • Per-request comment thread — the conversation lives on the request, not in an inbox.
  • Per-request activity log — a full audit trail for each request, which is §06 arriving from a completely different direction.
  • Dynamic list updated in real time, so nobody reconciles versions.
  • Multi-select and bulk action — accept, download or edit a group at once.
  • Dashboards so a manager reads status at a glance instead of asking for an update.
  • Templates and rollover — "Roll engagements over from year to year and capture all additional requests that came up during the engagement."

Issues and remediation — Diligent

Status (five defaults): New · Open · Under Review · Closed · False Positive
Priority (four predefined): Low · Medium · High · Critical

Plus Assignee, Workflow Group (governs who can see the record), Published date, Publisher name, Updated datetime, Closed datetime.

At engagement level their audit guide runs: captured → assigned to owner → Awaiting Management Response → remedial actions executed → retested → closed and ready for reporting.

  • "False Positive" as a first-class status is a small decision with real consequences: an explicit, recorded way to say this was not real, instead of deleting the record. Deleting destroys the trail; a status preserves it.
  • "Awaiting Management Response" as its own state makes visible that the ball is in the client's court — exactly what an audit committee wants counted separately from audit is behind.

C · The three mechanics worth stealing first

1 · A status-linked questionnaire is a sign-off gate that cannot be skipped

Straight from Diligent's documentation: "If a status is linked to a status questionnaire, you are prompted to answer a questionnaire when changing the record's status to the linked value." Read that again in the light of §04. This is human sign-off implemented as a data constraint rather than as a policy in a manual. Nobody — and no agent — can move an issue to Closed without a human answering the closure questions, and the answers are stored on the record as part of its history. It is the cheapest possible implementation of "the agent may not be the control", it is impossible to forget, and it demos in about thirty seconds. Steal this one first.

2 · Triggers are condition → action, with no model in the loop

Diligent's remediation triggers fire on new or updated records, optionally with conditions, and can escalate a record's priority to critical, assign it to a workflow group and a named user, and send out a questionnaire. Entirely declarative, fully auditable, and not a single generative step involved. Almost everything in Tier 1 that looks like intelligence is actually this. It is also the cheapest thing to build in a low-code environment, which is a happy result: the most convincing part of the demo costs the least to make.

3 · Rollover — the biggest saving in the category, and it uses no AI at all

Suralink's pitch rests on the observation that engagements are roughly the same year to year, so last cycle's request list should be next cycle's starting point, carrying forward the extra requests that surfaced along the way. The audit function's own history is the best available template for its next audit. A demo that opens with "here is last year's request list, already populated, with the twelve extra items you had to ask for after it started" lands harder than anything generative, because it is visibly their own work coming back improved.

§05 reached its Tier 1 / 2 / 3 ranking from the Standards — what the IIA assigns to a named human. This section reaches the same ranking from the market — what companies that would profit enormously from automating judgment have chosen not to ship. Two derivations from opposite directions agreeing is the strongest evidence in this document. It is also worth saying out loud in the room, because it reframes the caution in §04 from timidity into the same call every serious vendor in this category has already made.

Steps 1 to 5 contain no AI whatsoever. That is a feature rather than an embarrassment, and it is worth being direct about in the room. It means the demo has a working spine before a single model call is made; the agent has somewhere structured to write; and every agent action in step 6 lands on a record that already knows how to be audited. It also means that if the generative parts disappoint on the day, there is still a working product on the screen. Build the boring part first and the impressive part becomes both cheap and defensible.

APPENDIX · THE MARKET

What already exists, in three layers.

You asked what existing models are doing internal audit for banks. Three distinct layers, and the distinction between them is the useful part.

LayerWhoWhat it actually is
Big 4 audit platformsEY Canvas · KPMG Clara · PwC Aura/Halo · DeloitteThe largest recent move: on 7 Apr 2026 EY announced a global roll-out of enterprise-scale agentic AI in Assurance, embedding a multi-agent framework built with Microsoft Azure, Foundry and Fabric into EY Canvas — reaching 130,000 assurance professionals across 160,000 engagements in 150+ countries, with end-to-end coverage targeted for 2028. KPMG Clara is built on Azure AI Foundry; PwC runs Aura and Halo; Deloitte launched a global agentic network in 2025. The critical qualifier: these are overwhelmingly external audit platforms, used by the firm on its own engagements. They are not sold to a bank's internal audit function to run itself. Different buyer, different artifact, different room.
Internal-audit software vendorsAuditBoard · Workiva · Diligent One · Suralink · MindBridgeThe layer that does sell to internal audit, and the one worth studying rather than worrying about. AuditBoard covers workflow automation, issue management and dashboard reporting. Workiva covers the workflow end to end with strong evidence-gathering and sign-off tooling. Diligent One publishes the clearest data model of the group. Suralink does nothing but manage evidence request lists. MindBridge scores transactions across a full ledger. They have all landed on issue management, evidence gathering and workpaper narrative — the same Tier 1 and Tier 2 that §05 reaches independently from the Standards. §07 takes their workflows apart.
Platform layerMicrosoft Copilot Studio, Power Platform, Dataverse, Purview, Agent 365Where this build lives. The differentiator is not a feature — it is position: an agent built inside the bank's own tenant, on data that never leaves it, fitted to that function's actual working process rather than to a vendor's template. For an internal audit function at a smaller bank, buying a full suite is a procurement project measured in quarters; a tenant-native agent is not. A real structural advantage, worth naming.

The gap, stated carefully

Across everything found, the market is well served at the workflow layer — tracking issues, managing engagements, analysing transactions — and I found no product whose primary proposition is the engagement-level evidence chain for agent actions described in §06: per-action provenance, indexed to workpaper references, retained on the audit function's clock, and exportable to a party with no access to the tenant. That is an absence of evidence rather than evidence of absence — a fast-moving market, and a negative search result is weak. But it is consistent with where the vendors have aimed, and it is the slice most worth claiming.
